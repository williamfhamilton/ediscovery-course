Electronic Discovery — Week 1: Foundations
What is a large language model, actually?
Three hands-on stages. No lecture — you'll build the intuition yourself.
The claim this session tests

A foundation large language model is not a database, and it does not "know" or "understand" facts the way a person does. At its core, it is a trained mathematical function that (1) breaks text into small pieces called tokens, (2) represents each token as a list of numbers — a vector — positioned in a high-dimensional space so that similar tokens sit near each other, and (3) repeatedly predicts the single most statistically likely next token given everything that came before, one token at a time.

Everything a model generates — a case summary, a discovery response, a contract clause — is the output of that prediction loop running thousands of times. Nothing more is retrieved from an internal filing cabinet of "known facts."

Why an e-discovery class needs this

You will soon be evaluating whether a client's use of generative AI tools is a discoverable data source, whether an AI-drafted document is authentic, and why a model can confidently state something false. Every one of those questions turns on understanding that outputs are statistically generated, not retrieved. This primer builds the mechanical intuition before we return to those doctrinal questions in Module 2A.

1
Text is not read as words — it's chopped into chunks
Tokenization
Try it yourself
A model never sees a sentence the way you do. Before anything else happens, text is split into tokens — sometimes a whole common word, sometimes just a fragment of a rarer or longer word. Long or unusual words get carved into sub-word chunks that the model has seen often across its training data, even if those chunks aren't meaningful units to a human reader.
💬 Group discussion
Notice which words got split into orange chunks. Why would a model builder want to break rare words into smaller, more common pieces rather than treating every whole word as its own unit? What happens when the model encounters a word it has literally never seen before, split or not?
2
Every token becomes a point in space
Embeddings
Click any word
Each token is converted into a vector — a long list of numbers — that places it at a specific point in a high-dimensional space. Real models use hundreds or thousands of dimensions; below is a toy 2-dimensional version so you can literally see the pattern: words that tend to appear in similar contexts end up positioned near each other. That spatial closeness is the entire mechanism the model uses to represent "meaning."

Click "litigation" and then click "wolf." Why do their nearest neighbors look so different? Now consider: nobody hand-labeled these clusters as "legal terms" or "animals" — the positions emerge purely from statistical patterns of what appears near what in enormous amounts of text. What are the implications if two very different concepts happen to be used in similar surrounding language?
3
Generation is just: predict the next chunk, one at a time
Next-token prediction
Build a sentence by clicking
Once text is tokenized and each token has a vector, the model's entire job at generation time is to look at everything so far and output a probability distribution over what token is likely to come next — then pick one (usually the highest-probability option, sometimes a close second for variety) and repeat. Below is a toy version built from a small training set of sentences, so the probabilities are simplistic — but the mechanism is the real one, just scaled down from billions of examples to a few dozen.
This exact chunk never appeared in the toy training set, so there's no data to predict from — a real model doesn't hit a dead end here because its vectors let it generalize to combinations it never saw verbatim. That generalization is also exactly how it can confidently generate a plausible-sounding but false statement.
💬 Group discussion
At each step, the model isn't "deciding" the fact is true — it's reporting which chunk is statistically likeliest to come next given the training data's patterns. Where in that loop would a hallucinated case citation or a fabricated Bates number come from? Does anything in this mechanism check the output against ground truth before it's produced?
